Measuring whether it worked, on real data

30 min

Three checks in increasing order of discomfort, then a worked evaluation on our own annotation record and an honest account of the number we still do not have.

  • Does the quoted span exist? Make the model quote, then check mechanically
  • Do two models agree? One model is an opinion
  • Do two humans agree? This is the number your committee will ask for
  • Reading a kappa: under 0.40 poor, 0.40-0.60 moderate, 0.60-0.80 substantial, over 0.80 check your independence
  • A real number from our own pipeline: an auto-draft kappa of 0.384 on the red-line layer, 0.563 on the neighbouring one. The coders were not disagreeing with each other, they were agreeing with the model
  • A real confusion matrix – 34 / 2 / 9 / 207 across 252 passages, raw agreement 93%
  • The same data, two answers: 93% raw agreement against a kappa of 0.24, because 216 of the 252 are an easy no that chance alone would get right
  • Per layer, not overall: the model scores -0.02 against a human on red lines and +0.72 on nuclear signalling. One headline accuracy would have hidden both
  • And the number we still do not have: all of the above is expert re-annotation, not two independent coders. The blind pass has not come back
  • The two traps: the router trap and the anchoring trap

Design decisions

Why this edition is not the 5-day programme compressed

The 5-day programme is 32 contact hours. This is 12.5. You keep 39% of the time, and an honest redesign starts by naming what dies – compression at that ratio produces a tour, and a tour is the one thing the original explicitly refuses to be.

Three things that structurally cannot be ported

The Track A / Track B split

Needs a split room and two-hour blocks

In a single 150-minute session there is no room to run two rooms. Everyone is in one room all week, so the mixed-audience problem the tracks existed to solve returns and needs another answer.

The week-long team project

The original gives it about 8 hours of in-room time

Two hours a day across four days, and it is the spine of the original. There are no spare hours here at all.

Day 5 group presentations

Depends on the team project

And Day 5 is wanted for their projects and the kit – a better use of the last session for this audience anyway.

A constraint the original never faced. Adam Stulberg, 31 July: students will be "coming and going throughout the week", and as of 26 August it was still unconfirmed whether they register or just show up. A cumulative team deliverable cannot survive a drop-in audience. Anything requiring Day 2 to have happened strands whoever arrives on Day 3.

The replacement spine

One running worked example, carried by the instructor

In the room

The same question is taken one stage further each day, live. It is the through-line, and it survives anyone missing a day because the instructor carries it, not the students.

One personal artifact per student, built between sessions

Between sessions

Students have 21.5 hours between one session and the next – far more than the 2.5 they spend with you. That, not the contact time, is the real resource. Each day ends with one concrete overnight task on their own topic.

A pre-built fallback artifact, every day

Every day is self-contained

Each day opens with a 10-minute cold start and ships a fallback corpus, taxonomy or result so a newcomer can still do the hands-on. Arriving on Wednesday costs you nothing.

Measured against the right parent. This edition was first sized against the 32-hour 5-day programme. The closest delivered analogue is actually the Boston 3-evening edition of 4 February 2026 – 3 × 3 h = 9 contact hours. GATech has roughly 39% more time than Boston, not 39% of the 5-day. So the right move is to expand the Boston edition, not to amputate the 5-day one. Treat the 5-day as the ceiling and Boston as the floor.

Handout: the six-line methods block

Copy this into your paper and fill it in. If your methods section carries these six lines it will survive review; if it carries fewer, the question you get asked will be about whichever one is missing.

LineWhat goes in itThe failure it prevents
1
Question		The analytical question, stated so that someone else could ask it again in the same words.A result that cannot be re-derived because nobody knows what was being asked.
2
Query		The query verbatim, the database, the interface version, and the date you ran it.An unreproducible corpus. Databases change under you; the date is what makes the N defensible.
3
Corpus		N documents, N chunks, the source mix, the languages, and exactly how duplicates were handled.A duplicate rate silently inflating every downstream count.
4
Scheme		Dimensions, levels, definitions, and one worked coding decision including a borderline case you rejected.A reader who cannot tell what your categories exclude, and therefore cannot tell what your finding means.
5
Reliability		Kappa and raw agreement, the base rate, how many coders, and whether they were blind to each other and to the model.Reporting 93% agreement on a corpus that is 86% one class. State both numbers or neither.
6
Blind spot		What your instrument cannot see, in one sentence, in your own words.A reviewer finding it first. Naming it yourself converts a weakness into a demonstration of control.
Line 5 is the one people get wrong. On our own red-line data the same 252 passages give 93% raw agreement and a kappa of 0.24. Both are correctly computed. Quoting only the first is the most common reliability error in this literature, and it is not usually dishonest – it is just stopping one line too early.

When it breaks

  • 40,000 hits. The query is a topic, not a question. Add the AND terms that the on-domain half of the record actually uses.
  • 11 hits. The query is a sentence. Drop the modifiers and widen before you conclude the literature does not exist.
  • Everything labelled the same. The taxonomy has one usable level. Split the dominant class before you classify anything more.
  • Coders disagreeing constantly. Usually the definitions, not the coders. Read the two most-disagreed items out loud and fix the wording.
  • Kappa suspiciously high. Check independence. If the coders saw the model answer, you measured compliance, not reliability.
The number worth quoting. Our own auto-draft kappa was 0.384. It was low because the coders were not disagreeing with each other, they were agreeing with the model. It is a measured number from our own pipeline rather than a borrowed illustration, which is exactly what earns the blind-coding argument.
