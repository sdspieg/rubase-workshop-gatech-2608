Out of the Dark Ages?

Supercharging your research workflow with LLMs. Five lunchtime sessions – lunch provided. One research pipeline is taken apart – from the question, through the corpus and the instrument, to a result you can defend – and put back together with tools that did not exist when most of our methods were written.

Sam Nunn School of International Affairs · 31 August – 4 September 2026 · 11:00–13:30 daily · Nunn Conference Room · 12.5 contact hours

Day 1 is the diagnosis. The rest of the week is the treatment. Monday names three things the field does badly – it disregards empirics, it disregards recall, and it disregards systematic reasoning. Each of the next three days repairs one of them.

12:05

Measuring whether it worked, on real data

30 min

Three checks in increasing order of discomfort, then a worked evaluation on our own annotation record and an honest account of the number we still do not have.

  • Does the quoted span exist? Make the model quote, then check mechanically
  • Do two models agree? One model is an opinion
  • Do two humans agree? This is the number your committee will ask for
  • Reading a kappa (agreement between two coders, corrected for chance): see RedLineBench for a worked case – under 0.40 poor, 0.40-0.60 moderate, 0.60-0.80 substantial, over 0.80 check your independence
  • A real number from our own pipeline: an auto-draft kappa of 0.384 on the red-line layer, 0.563 on the neighboring one. The coders were not disagreeing with each other, they were agreeing with the model
  • A real confusion matrix – 34 / 2 / 9 / 207 across 252 passages, raw agreement 93%
  • The same data, two answers: 93% raw agreement against a kappa of 0.24, because 216 of the 252 are an easy no that chance alone would get right
  • Per layer, not overall: the model scores -0.02 against a human on red lines and +0.72 on nuclear signalling. One headline accuracy would have hidden both
  • And the number we still do not have: all of the above is expert re-annotation, not two independent coders. The blind pass has not come back
  • The two traps: the router trap and the anchoring trap

Prompts you can reuse

These are the actual production prompts, with the domain left blank for you. They are not written for teaching, which is why they are worth having: what looks like excess strictness in them is each a failure someone already had.

  • Generating a taxonomy · the MDTDF prompt ↗Fill in one domain and paste it whole. The section you must not skip is the independence test – a model will happily hand you a beautiful taxonomy whose dimensions are not independent, and it will not warn you.
  • Classifying a corpus · the two-phase prompt ↗Gatekeeping first, classification second. Most of your quality comes from phase 1: given categories and no exit, a model will classify everything you show it.

Handout: the six-line methods block

Copy this into your paper and fill it in. If your methods section carries these six lines it will survive review; if it carries fewer, the question you get asked will be about whichever one is missing.

LineWhat goes in itThe failure it prevents
1
Question		The analytical question, stated so that someone else could ask it again in the same words.A result that cannot be re-derived because nobody knows what was being asked.
2
Query		The query verbatim, the database, the interface version, and the date you ran it.An unreproducible corpus. Databases change under you; the date is what makes the N defensible.
3
Corpus		N documents, N chunks, the source mix, the languages, and exactly how duplicates were handled.A duplicate rate silently inflating every downstream count.
4
Scheme		Dimensions, levels, definitions, and one worked coding decision including a borderline case you rejected.A reader who cannot tell what your categories exclude, and therefore cannot tell what your finding means.
5
Reliability		Kappa and raw agreement, the base rate, how many coders, and whether they were blind to each other and to the model.Reporting 93% agreement on a corpus that is 86% one class. State both numbers or neither.
6
Blind spot		What your instrument cannot see, in one sentence, in your own words.A reviewer finding it first. Naming it yourself converts a weakness into a demonstration of control.
Line 5 is the one people get wrong. On our own red-line data the same 252 passages give 93% raw agreement and a kappa of 0.24. Both are correctly computed. Quoting only the first is the most common reliability error in this literature, and it is not usually dishonest – it is just stopping one line too early.

The words we use

Ten terms, in plain English

Every one of these gets explained properly in the room. This page is here so that nothing on this site is a wall you cannot get over, and so you can check a word on Thursday that you first met on Monday.

Corpus

also called: corpora

The set of documents you are working from, assembled on purpose rather than whatever a search happened to return. Building one is Tuesday.

Taxonomy

A scheme of categories, arranged in levels, that you apply to those documents. It is an instrument: it decides what your study can and cannot see. Building one is Wednesday.

Ontology

A step beyond a taxonomy. A taxonomy says a tiger is a mammal; an ontology also says a tiger preys on deer. It records relationships, not just categories.

Classification

also called: coding, annotation

Applying your taxonomy to each document or passage, by hand or by model, so that a pile of text becomes something you can count. Thursday.

Precision and recall

Two ways a search can fail. Precision is how much of what you found is actually relevant; recall is how much of what exists you actually found. Chasing one usually costs you the other.

Cohen's kappa

also called: kappa, inter-annotator agreement

A number between roughly 0 and 1 for how much two coders agree beyond what chance alone would give them. It matters because plain agreement looks flattering when most items belong to one category. Thursday shows the same data scoring 93% agreement and a kappa of 0.24.

Confusion matrix

The small table behind that number: how often both coders said yes, both said no, and each one disagreed. Reading it tells you which direction the disagreement runs.

Bibliometrics

Studying the published record itself – who publishes, where, with whom, on what, and when – rather than reading each paper. It is how you see the shape of a field.

RAG

also called: retrieval-augmented generation

Giving a language model a specific set of documents to answer from, instead of relying on whatever it absorbed in training. It reduces invention; it does not eliminate it.

LLM

also called: large language model

The kind of model behind ChatGPT, Claude and their relatives. In this workshop it is an instrument to be calibrated and checked, not an oracle.

If a word is used in the room and you do not know it, say so. Every term above was jargon to everyone present at some point, and the person who asks is usually asking for four other people as well.
